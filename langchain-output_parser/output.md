## Executive Summary

The **Big Bang Theory** is the prevailing cosmological model explaining the origin, evolution, and large-scale structure of the observable universe. According to this model, the universe expanded from an extremely hot, dense, and compressed state approximately **13.8 billion years ago**. Rather than describing an explosion *in* space, the Big Bang describes the continuous expansion *of* space itself. 

This report provides a comprehensive examination of the Big Bang Theory, tracing its historical origin, outlining its timeline, reviewing the empirical pillars supporting it, addressing common misconceptions, and exploring unresolved cosmological mysteries.

---

## 1. Historical Background and Development

For centuries, the static model—the idea that the universe had no beginning and remains unchanging—was the dominant scientific view. The shift toward the Big Bang model occurred through key theoretical and observational breakthroughs in the 20th century:

* **Albert Einstein (1915):** Published the *General Theory of Relativity*, which provided the geometric framework for gravity and spacetime. Though Einstein initially introduced a "cosmological constant" to keep his equations aligned with a static universe, his equations naturally implied dynamic space (expanding or contracting).
* **Georges Lemaître (1927):** A Belgian physicist and Catholic priest, Lemaître independently derived solutions to Einstein's field equations and proposed that the universe began from a single point, which he termed the "Primeval Atom" or "Cosmic Egg."
* **Edwin Hubble (1929):** Provided crucial observational proof using the Hooker Telescope at Mount Wilson Observatory. Hubble discovered that distant galaxies are moving away from Earth at velocities proportional to their distance. This relationship, known as **Hubble’s Law**, confirmed that the universe is actively expanding.

---

## 2. Chronological Timeline of Cosmic Evolution

Cosmologists divide the history of the universe into distinct epochs based on energy levels, temperature, and physical processes.

```
[Singularity] ➔ [Planck Epoch] ➔ [Inflation] ➔ [Electroweak/Hadron Epochs] ➔ [Nucleosynthesis] ➔ [Recombination (CMBR)] ➔ [Dark Ages & Structure Formation]
```

### A. The Very Early Universe ($0$ to $10^{-32}$ seconds)
* **The Planck Epoch ($0$ to $10^{-43}$ seconds):** The initial state where temperatures approached $10^{32}$ Kelvin. Quantum effects dominated, and all four fundamental forces (Gravity, Electromagnetism, Strong Nuclear Force, Weak Nuclear Force) were unified into a single force. Current physics cannot describe this state without a unified theory of Quantum Gravity.
* **Cosmic Inflation ($10^{-36}$ to $10^{-32}$ seconds):** Driven by a high-energy field, the universe underwent an exponential expansion, growing by a factor of at least $10^{26}$ in a fraction of a second. Inflation explains why the universe appears spatially flat and uniform on large scales.

### B. The Early Universe ($10^{-32}$ seconds to 380,000 years)
* **Hadron and Lepton Epochs ($10^{-6}$ to 1 second):** As the universe cooled, energy condensed into matter. Quarks combined to form hadrons (protons and neutrons), followed by the creation of leptons (electrons, neutrinos).
* **Big Bang Nucleosynthesis (1 minute to 20 minutes):** Temperatures dropped to roughly one billion Kelvin, allowing protons and neutrons to fuse. This phase produced the primordial chemical composition of the universe: approximately **75% Hydrogen, 25% Helium**, and trace amounts of Lithium and Deuterium.
* **Recombination and Photon Decoupling (~380,000 years):** The universe cooled to roughly 3,000 Kelvin, allowing free electrons to bind with atomic nuclei to form neutral atoms. Photons, which were previously trapped in a dense plasma of free charges, were suddenly able to travel freely through space. This released light is observed today as the **Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)**.

### C. Structure Formation (380,000 years to Present)
* **The Dark Ages and Reionization:** For hundreds of millions of years, no light-emitting sources existed. Over time, subtle density fluctuations left behind by inflation caused matter to pool gravitationally. 
* **Stellar and Galactic Genesis:** The first stars (Population III) ignited roughly 100 to 200 million years after the Big Bang, reionizing surrounding gas. Gravitational collapse continued, leading to the formation of galaxies, galaxy clusters, and planetary systems.

## 3. Key Empirical Pillars Supporting the Theory

The Big Bang model is widely accepted by the scientific community because it is corroborated by independent lines of observational evidence:

```
└─────────────────┘             └─────────────────┘             └─────────────────┘
```

### 1. Cosmological Redshift
When light travels across expanding space, its wavelength stretches toward the red end of the electromagnetic spectrum. Observations demonstrate that light from distant galaxies is systematically redshifted, and the magnitude of redshift increases directly with distance, confirming continuous spatial expansion.

### 2. Cosmic Microwave Background Radiation (CMBR)
Discovered accidentally in 1965 by Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, the CMB is a uniform thermal radiation filling the universe, currently cooled by expansion to **2.725 Kelvin**. Advanced space telescopes (COBE, WMAP, and Planck) mapped tiny fluctuations ($1$ part in $100,000$) in this radiation, providing a snapshot of the early universe's density seeds that grew into galaxies.

### 3. Abundance of Light Elements
Theoretical calculations of Big Bang Nucleosynthesis accurately predict the ratio of lighter elements observed in primordial, undisturbed regions of space (such as old dwarf galaxies and intergalactic gas clouds). The observed cosmic ratios ($75\% \text{ H}, 24\% \text{ He-4}$, along with fractions of Deuterium and Helium-3) match predicted values precisely.

### 4. Galactic Evolution and Large-Scale Structure
Deep-field observations by instruments like the Hubble Space Telescope and the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST) confirm that galaxies located billions of light-years away look younger, smaller, and less structurally evolved than nearby galaxies. This refutes steady-state models and proves the universe evolves over time.

## 4. Addressing Common Misconceptions

| Misconception | Scientific Reality |
| :--- | :--- |
| **"The Big Bang was an explosion in space."** | It was an expansion **of** space. Matter was not propelled outward into an existing void; rather, space itself expanded, carrying matter along with it. |
| **"The universe expanded from a central point."** | The Big Bang happened **everywhere at once**. Because space itself expanded, every point in the universe perceives itself as expanding away from every other point. |
| **"The theory explains the literal creation of everything from nothing."** | The model describes how the universe evolved from a dense, high-energy initial state. It does not explain the origin of the initial state itself or events prior to the Planck epoch. |

## 5. Unresolved Questions and Frontiers in Modern Cosmology

While the Big Bang model successfully accounts for much of the observed universe, significant questions remain open at the frontiers of physics:

1. **Dark Matter:** Observations show that visible matter accounts for only about 5% of the universe's total energy density. Roughly 27% consists of "Dark Matter"—an unidentified non-baryonic matter that interacts via gravity but does not absorb, reflect, or emit light.
2. **Dark Energy:** In 1998, observations of Type Ia supernovae revealed that the expansion rate of the universe is **accelerating**, rather than slowing down under gravity. This accelerated growth is attributed to "Dark Energy," an unknown form of energy making up approximately 68% of the universe.
3. **The Singularity Problem:** Applying General Relativity backward in time leads to a point of infinite density and temperature—a mathematical singularity. A complete theory of **Quantum Gravity** (such as String Theory or Loop Quantum Gravity) is required to understand the conditions at $t = 0$.
4. **Baryon Asymmetry:** Standard particle physics predicts that equal amounts of matter and antimatter should have been created during the Big Bang. However, the observable universe consists almost entirely of matter. The underlying mechanism responsible for this imbalance remains an active area of research.

The Big Bang Theory is one of the greatest achievements of modern science. Supported by robust observational evidence—including cosmic redshift, the Cosmic Microwave Background, and the abundance of primordial elements—it provides a coherent narrative for how a hot, dense state evolved into the vast cosmos observed today. 

As advanced instruments like the James Webb Space Telescope, the Vera C.  Here is the pseudocode for **Radix Sort** (using Least Significant Digit / LSD approach), along with its helper function, **Counting Sort**, which is used to stably sort the digits at each position.

### Main Algorithm: Radix Sort

```text
Algorithm RadixSort(A):
    Input: An array A of n non-negative integers
    Output: The array A sorted in ascending order

    // Step 1: Find the maximum number to know the number of digits
    max_value = FindMax(A)

    // Step 2: Do counting sort for every digit.
    // Instead of passing digit number, exp (exponent) is passed.
    // exp is 10^0 (1s place), 10^1 (10s place), 10^2 (100s place), etc.
    exp = 1
    while (max_value / exp) > 0:
        CountingSortByDigit(A, exp)
        exp = exp * 10

Function FindMax(A):
    max_val = A[0]
    for i = 1 to length(A) - 1:
        if A[i] > max_val:
            max_val = A[i]
    return max_val
```

---

### Helper Subroutine: Counting Sort by Digit

```text
Algorithm CountingSortByDigit(A, exp):
    Input: Array A, Exponent exp representing the digit position
    
    n = length(A)
    output = Array of size n
    count = Array of size 10, initialized with all 0s  // Base 10 digits (0 to 9)

    // Step 1: Count occurrences of each digit at (A[i] / exp) % 10
    for i = 0 to n - 1:
        digit = floor(A[i] / exp) mod 10
        count[digit] = count[digit] + 1

    // Step 2: Change count[i] so that it contains the actual 
    // position of this digit in output[]
    for i = 1 to 9:
        count[i] = count[i] + count[i - 1]

    // Step 3: Build the output array 
    // (Iterate BACKWARDS to maintain stability of the sort)
    for i = n - 1 down to 0:
        digit = floor(A[i] / exp) mod 10
        output[count[digit] - 1] = A[i]
        count[digit] = count[digit] - 1

    // Step 4: Copy the output array back to A
    for i = 0 to n - 1:
        A[i] = output[i]
```

### Complexity Analysis

* **Time Complexity:** 
  * **$O(d \cdot (n + k))$**, where:
    * $n$ = Number of elements in the array.
    * $d$ = Number of digits in the maximum number (i.e., $\log_{10}(\text{max\_value})$).
    * $k$ = Range of digits (for base 10, $k = 10$).
  * If $d$ is constant, Radix Sort runs in **linear time $O(n)$**.

* **Space Complexity:** 
  * **$O(n + k)$** due to the temporary `output` array of size $n$ and `count` array of size $k$ used in Counting Sort.

### 1. Rust Implementation

In Rust, we mutate the slice in place (`&mut [u32]`). We handle potential empty slices gracefully and use standard Rust constructs like `.copy_from_slice()` and checked multiplication to prevent integer overflow.

```rust
/// Main function to perform Radix Sort on a slice of non-negative integers
pub fn radix_sort(arr: &mut [u32]) {
    if arr.is_empty() {
        return;
    }

    // Step 1: Find the maximum number to know the number of digits
    let max_value = *arr.iter().max().unwrap();

    // Step 2: Do counting sort for every digit.
    // exp is 10^0, 10^1, 10^2, etc.
    let mut exp = 1u32;
    while max_value / exp > 0 {
        counting_sort_by_digit(arr, exp);
        
        // Prevent integer overflow on multiplication
        match exp.checked_mul(10) {
            Some(next_exp) => exp = next_exp,
            None => break,
        }
    }
}

/// Helper Subroutine: Counting Sort by Digit
fn counting_sort_by_digit(arr: &mut [u32], exp: u32) {
    let n = arr.len();
    let mut output = vec![0u32; n];
    let mut count = [0usize; 10]; // Base 10 digits (0 to 9)

    // Step 1: Count occurrences of each digit
    for &num in arr.iter() {
        let digit = ((num / exp) % 10) as usize;
        count[digit] += 1;
    }

    // Step 2: Change count[i] so that it contains actual position in output[]
    for i in 1..10 {
        count[i] += count[i - 1];
    }

    // Step 3: Build the output array (Iterate BACKWARDS to maintain stability)
    for i in (0..n).rev() {
        let digit = ((arr[i] / exp) % 10) as usize;
        output[count[digit] - 1] = arr[i];
        count[digit] -= 1;
    }

    // Step 4: Copy the output array back to arr
    arr.copy_from_slice(&output);
}

fn main() {
    let mut numbers = vec![170, 45, 75, 90, 802, 24, 2, 66];
    println!("Original array: {:?}", numbers);

    radix_sort(&mut numbers);

    println!("Sorted array:   {:?}", numbers);
}
```

### 2. Ruby Implementation

In Ruby, we follow the convention of appending `!` to method names that mutate the receiver in place.

```ruby
# Helper Subroutine: Counting Sort by Digit
def counting_sort_by_digit!(arr, exp)
  n = arr.length
  output = Array.new(n, 0)
  count = Array.new(10, 0) # Base 10 digits (0 to 9)

  # Step 1: Count occurrences of each digit
  arr.each do |num|
    digit = (num / exp) % 10
    count[digit] += 1
  end

  # Step 2: Change count[i] so that it contains the actual position in output[]
  (1..9).each do |i|
    count[i] += count[i - 1]
  end

  # Step 3: Build the output array (Iterate BACKWARDS to maintain stability)
  (n - 1).downto(0) do |i|
    digit = (arr[i] / exp) % 10
    output[count[digit] - 1] = arr[i]
    count[digit] -= 1
  end

  # Step 4: Copy output array back to arr
  (0...n).each do |i|
    arr[i] = output[i]
  end
end

# Main Function: Radix Sort
def radix_sort!(arr)
  return arr if arr.empty?

  # Step 1: Find the maximum number to know the number of digits
  max_value = arr.max

  # Step 2: Do counting sort for every digit position
  exp = 1
  while max_value / exp > 0
    counting_sort_by_digit!(arr, exp)
    exp *= 10
  end

  arr
end
```
